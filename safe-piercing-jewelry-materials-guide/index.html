JEWELRY SAFETY

Why Jewelry Material Matters Most

Titanium, steel, and gold: what experts say about choosing safe piercing jewelry and avoiding toxic metal risks.

Published: August 17, 2026

THE STORY

Material Literacy Becomes Part of Piercing Safety

As piercing culture in the U.S. has expanded beyond traditional gold studs, the materials used in piercing jewelry have become a bigger part of the conversation than the designs themselves. Reports of allergic reactions, prolonged irritation, and even metal toxicity concerns tied to low-quality jewelry have pushed both consumers and piercing professionals to pay closer attention to material sourcing and certification, rather than just appearance or price.

This is especially relevant in the U.S. market, where jewelry sold for piercings — particularly through mass retailers and online marketplaces — is not subject to uniform federal material-safety standards specific to body jewelry. That gap means the responsibility largely falls on consumers and piercers to verify quality, making material literacy an important, if underdiscussed, part of piercing safety.

RECOMMENDED

Materials Professionals Recommend

Implant-Grade Titanium

Titanium that meets ASTM F136 or ISO 5832-3 standards is widely recommended by piercing professionals, including those affiliated with the Association of Professional Piercers, for both new and healed piercings. It is prized for being nickel-free and biocompatible, meaning it's manufactured to standards originally developed for surgical implants.

Certified Surgical Steel (ASTM F138 / ISO 5832-1)

Surgical steel that meets recognized implant-grade certifications can be a safe option for many wearers, though it typically contains trace nickel, which can still trigger reactions in individuals with nickel sensitivity. Buyers should look specifically for ASTM F138 or ISO 5832-1 certification rather than generic "surgical steel" labeling, which is not a regulated term in the U.S.

Solid Gold (14k or Higher)

Solid 14k or higher gold is considered safe for healed piercings by many professionals, provided it's genuinely solid rather than gold-plated. Gold-plated or gold-filled jewelry is generally discouraged for new or healing piercings, since plating can wear away and expose base metals underneath, some of which may contain nickel or other reactive alloys.

AVOID

Materials to Avoid

Nickel-Heavy Alloys

Nickel is one of the most common causes of contact allergies from jewelry, and low-cost piercing jewelry — often sold without clear material certification — can contain higher nickel content than labeled. The FDA has previously flagged nickel allergy as a recognized consumer skin-sensitivity concern, though enforcement around body jewelry labeling specifically remains limited.

Uncertified "Surgical Steel" or "Hypoallergenic" Jewelry

Terms like "hypoallergenic" and "surgical steel" are commonly used in marketing but are not strictly regulated in the U.S. body jewelry market, meaning products carrying these labels aren't guaranteed to meet implant-grade standards. Consumers should look for specific material certifications rather than relying on marketing language alone.

Novelty and Costume Metals

Jewelry made from unspecified "mixed metal" or costume alloys, sometimes found in inexpensive novelty piercing sets, carries a higher risk of containing lead, cadmium, or other metals considered unsafe for prolonged skin contact. These materials are particularly inappropriate for new or healing piercings, where the skin barrier is compromised.

Why This Matters Beyond Comfort

Piercing professionals emphasize that jewelry material choice isn't just about avoiding itching or discomfort — prolonged exposure to reactive metals in an open piercing channel can contribute to chronic irritation, delayed healing, or scarring in some cases. This is a widely held professional position, though individual outcomes vary and readers experiencing persistent irritation should consult a healthcare provider or dermatologist rather than self-diagnose.

Note: This article summarizes commonly cited industry certification standards and is not medical advice. Consult a healthcare provider for reaction or allergy concerns.

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the safest metal for a new piercing?

Implant-grade titanium (ASTM F136) is widely recommended by piercing professionals for new piercings due to its biocompatibility and nickel-free composition.

Can I wear gold-plated jewelry in a new piercing?

Most professionals discourage gold-plated jewelry for new piercings since the plating can wear away and expose reactive base metals.

Is "surgical steel" always safe?

Not necessarily — the term isn't strictly regulated, so look for ASTM F138 or ISO 5832-1 certification specifically.

What are signs of a metal allergy in a piercing?

Persistent redness, itching, or irritation localized to the jewelry area can suggest a reaction; consult a healthcare provider for evaluation.

Is 14k gold safe for piercings?

Solid 14k or higher gold is generally considered safe for healed piercings by many professionals, provided it is genuinely solid gold.
